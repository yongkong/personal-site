## Background

learn-mattpocock-skills is a bilingual course site teaching AI-native
development workflows ("Agent Skills 中文课"). The audience is
Chinese-speaking developers moving from "I use AI to autocomplete" to "AI
is part of a disciplined process." The constraint was brutal and familiar:
one person, no design team, and a reason to ship *this week* — content
sites that launch stale never launch.

## My role

Solo, end to end: spec, information architecture, content, design,
implementation, and deployment. That sounds like a lot until you separate
what the AI-native workflow actually requires from a human — deciding,
mostly — from what it doesn't — typing, mostly.

## Technical decisions

Next.js + TypeScript + MDX, statically exported. Content lives in MDX with
build-time validation: a post with a missing title or date fails the build
rather than rendering broken. The delivery method mirrored the course
subject:

1. a short spec — positioning, sections, what's explicitly out of scope
2. tracer-bullet tickets, each a vertical slice with acceptance criteria
3. one pre-agreed testing seam (the rendered static output) with
   scriptable assertions, so "done" was checkable by a machine
4. code review on every diff — including the AI-written parts, *especially*
   the AI-written parts

The stack was chosen for restartability, not novelty: everything I already
had proof I could ship fast.

Idea to deployed static site in a week, working evenings. What the
workflow made genuinely cheap: scaffolding, boilerplate, the MDX pipeline,
first drafts of repetitive structure. What stayed expensive, correctly:
positioning, ordering of ideas, and the final read of every sentence a
learner would see. The receipts live in the repository — build logs,
assertion counts, commit history — which is the point: claims about an
AI-native workflow should be auditable, not aspirational.
